Korean Dream Interpretation (해몽): A Practical Guide

해몽 (haemong) — Korean dream interpretation — isn't a fringe belief kept alive by a handful of enthusiasts. It's an ordinary, everyday piece of Korean life: grandmothers reading a grandchild's dream over breakfast, coworkers half-joking about buying a lottery ticket after a good one, national news outlets running matter-of-fact stories about jackpot winners who say they saw it coming the night before. It sits alongside Saju as one of two major, distinct fortune-telling traditions in Korean culture — where Saju reads the moment you were born, 해몽 reads what your sleeping mind produced last night.

The tradition has its own genre of story that's worth knowing about honestly rather than either dismissing or overselling. Alongside the lottery-winner stories, there's an equally common genre of disaster-avoidance stories — someone who cancels a trip, skips a flight, or turns down a ride after an unsettling dream, and the vehicle or plan they avoided runs into real trouble. These stories circulate constantly in Korean media and online communities, and they're genuinely compelling to read. It's worth noticing, the same way it's worth noticing with the lottery stories, that nobody publishes a story about the disaster dream that led to nothing — which is what happens to the overwhelming majority of unsettling dreams. The stories that get retold are the rare, dramatic hits, not a representative sample of what dreams actually predict.

None of that makes 해몽 worthless — it makes it exactly as honest a tradition as Saju is on this site: not a prediction engine, but a genuinely old, genuinely rich shared language for naming feelings that are otherwise hard to name. A culture doesn't keep a tradition alive for centuries because it's literally accurate; it keeps it alive because it's useful for talking about hope, dread, grief, and family in a shared vocabulary everyone already understands.
